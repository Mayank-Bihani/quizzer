For centuries the semi-arid plains of the northern Deccan relied not on rivers but on a dense mesh of human-made tanks — shallow reservoirs that captured monsoon run-off and released it through sluices as the dry season wore on. Colonial administrators, encountering this system in the nineteenth century, tended to read it as a patchwork of isolated ponds rather than what it was: a cascading network in which the overflow of one tank fed the inlet of the next, maintained by village councils that assigned desilting duties, adjudicated disputes over sluice timing, and financed repairs from a share of the harvest.

The arrival of large dams and canal-fed irrigation after independence did not simply add capacity to this older system; it also removed the fiscal and social logic that had kept the tanks in repair, since a farmer who could draw water from a canal had little reason to fund the upkeep of a tank three villages upstream. The result, several agrarian historians now argue, was not modernisation cleanly displacing tradition but one commons regime being dismantled before its replacement could cover the same ground — leaving a visible gap in drought years that the canal network was never sized to fill.
